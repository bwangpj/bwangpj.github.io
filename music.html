“Cramer's widow communicates a pleasant anecdote. At an Augarten Concert the two pianists were walking together and hearing a performance of Mozart's Pianoforte Concerto [No. 24] in C minor; Beethoven suddenly stood still and, directing his companion's attention to the exceedingly simple, but equally beautiful motive which is first introduced towards the end of the piece, exclaimed: ‘Cramer, Cramer! we shall never be able to do anything like that!’ As the theme was repeated and wrought up to the climax, Beethoven, swaying his body to and fro, marked the time and in every possible manner manifested a delight rising to enthusiasm.” — Thayer's Life of Beethoven

Qui tollis


“To my dear friend Haydn,
A father who had resolved to send his children out into the great world took it to be his duty to confide them to the protection and guidance of a very celebrated Man, especially when the latter by good fortune was at the same time his best Friend. Here they are then, O great Man and my dearest Friend, these six children of mine. They are, it is true, the fruit of a long and laborious endeavour, yet the hope inspired in me by several Friends that my efforts may be at least somewhat rewarded encourages me, and I flatter myself that this offspring will serve to afford me some solace one day. [...] May it therefore please you to receive them kindly and to be their Father, Guide and Friend! From this moment I resign to you all my rights in them, begging you however to look indulgently upon the defects which the partiality of a Father’s eye may have concealed from me, and in spite of them to continue in your generous Friendship for him who so greatly values it, while I remain, Dearest Friend, with all my heart, your most Sincere Friend,
W. A. Mozart”
— Mozart's dedication to Haydn of his set of six string quartets, of which the last one starts in a rather special way


“What a loss to the musical world!” — Mozart in a letter to his father, on the death of J. C. Bach (youngest son of the more famous Bach), who taught Mozart when he was much younger. Later that year Mozart made a touching quotation of one of J. C. Bach's overtures in the second movement of his Piano Concerto No. 12

“The fact that people generally don't understand or respect the very best things, such as Mozart's concertos and the aforementioned violin concerto of Viotti, is what sustains us and brings us fame. “Mahler saved Mozart from the lie of daintiness as well as from the boredom of academic dryness. He imparted to the performances the composer's dramatic seriousness, his sincerity, his vivacity. He thus managed to change the public's inanimate respect for Mozart operas into an enthusiasm that shook the house and enraptured even the sober heart of the box-office manager.” — Bruno Walter's autobiography.

“If you are poor, but skilful, then arm yourself with patience, and work hard; if you do not become rich, you do at least remain a skilful man. If you are a donkey, but rich, then make use of your advantage, be lazy; if you do not become poor, you do at least remain a donkey.” — from a pamphlet of ‘proverbs’ written by Mozart for a ball in 1786


“[...] he never sought the approval of the crowd; even as a child, only the praise of experts moved him. Therefore, he was indifferent to those who came to gawk at him out of mere curiosity. Perhaps this behavior often went too far. Consequently, he was sometimes unwilling to play even in the presence of great dignitaries of the highest rank; or he would play nothing but trifles when he realized they were neither experts nor true enthusiasts. But Mozart was the most agreeable man in the world when he saw that someone appreciated his art; he would play for hours for the humblest, often unknown, person. With encouraging attentiveness, he listened to the attempts of young artists and awakened their dormant self-confidence with affectionate applause. Our finest pianist and beloved composer, Johann Witassek, owes him this awakening of his talent. He himself confessed that the few hours he spent with Mozart greatly enhanced his training.” — from the first full biography of Mozart by Niemetschek (1808)

“Give me the best clavier in Europe, but people as listeners who understand nothing, or do not wish to understand anything, and who do not join with me in feeling what I play, I will then lose all joy.” — Mozart to his father, May 1778

“ How does Haydn hear and utter the name Mozart?
